Sable did not argue. That was the first bad sign.

She opened the ledger against her forearm and turned a page with a wet thumb. She did not read anything out. She only looked. Maren had watched men find their own names on a list before. Their faces all did the same thing.

"Vosk," Sable said. "At what hour did you arrive at this market?"

"Before the gulls."

"That is not an hour."

"Got nothing better."

The young constable wrote it down anyway. His pencil was a stub and he licked the tip to make it last. Words were cheap. Ink was cheaper. Chains cost.

Sable closed the ledger. "Second tide bell."

"What happens then."

"You will present yourself at the barracks on Warden Row. You will ask for me. Not the desk sergeant. Me." A pause, small and exact. "You will bring what you declined to carry."

"And if we don't?"

"Then the writ is served by other means, and I will not be the one serving it." She said it the way a clerk reads a cargo weight. No pleasure in it. That was worse than pleasure.

Tovin's breathing went shallow. Maren heard it without looking.

"Constable Pell has provided an account of your morning," Sable said. "It is a poor account. It has holes in it the size of gulls."

"Look, Warden, I only said what I saw, and what I saw was—"

"Ten silver marks." Sable did not raise her voice. "Three days in the barracks. That is the price of a false account. I am — deferring collection."

Tovin's mouth stayed open around nothing.

Maren shifted her weight. Her left hand had gone numb again, the way it did since the night in the cooper's cellar. She'd paid for that. Paid twice. Cost her a morning and most of a day's strength and she still tasted copper when the rain came on too fast. Nobody here knew that. Nobody here was going to.

"That's a lot of rope," she said, "for one fish gutter."

"You are not a fish gutter." Sable's eyes came up. Rain ran off her hat brim in a clean line and did not touch her face. "You are a name in a book. Names move more easily while they are still walking. I would prefer you walking."

"Prefer."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Another pause. Longer. Somewhere behind Sable's face a door opened and shut.

"Because the alternative is paperwork," she said, "and paperwork belongs to the Council."

She put the ledger under her arm, the way a person carries a child. Or a knife.

"Constable."

The young one looked up. He looked at Maren. His mouth moved. Whatever was in there, he decided against it, and Maren marked his face for later — bad shave, good cloak, a man who almost said a true thing in front of his Warden.

Then they went. Gray backs into gray rain. The crowd closed behind them and pretended it had not opened.

Maren counted to twenty. The fishwives went back to shouting. A gull took something off a slab and left with it.

Tovin sat down on the crate. He put his hands on his knees. They were shaking and he let them.

"Look," he said. "Maren. Look, I have to tell you a thing and you are going to be angry, and you have every right, and I would rather say it now than have it come out later, because it will come out later, it always does, and—"

"Tovin."

"—she asked me before you came. Earlier. By the ice house. She had the book open and she asked me where you were sleeping and I said the Anchor, and that was true, you were at the Anchor, so it was not a lie, it was only not the whole—"

"Stop."

He stopped. He wiped his face with the flat of his hand.

"Ten marks," he said. "I don't have ten marks. I don't have two. Sorry. That is not an apology, that is just arithmetic."

Maren looked at the tide board. One bell had gone. The second was maybe two hours out, and the water was already turning, and the ferry from the Reach had sailed back at half ebb.

Her sister had written three words on oilcloth three weeks ago and then gone quiet. Now there was a name in a Warden's book and a name in a sealed writ and a woman who wanted her walking instead of dragged.

Two hours. Enough time to reach Warden Row. Enough time to reach the Tide Isles boat if Halloran still owed her. Not enough for both.

"Get up," Maren said. "We're not standing here."

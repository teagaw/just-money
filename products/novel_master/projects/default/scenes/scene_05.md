Tovin's mouth closed. He looked at his slightly torn boots.

"Everything," he said. "The green-shawl woman. The ferry from the Reach. That we don't sleep much. Look, she asks a question like she already has the answer written down, and staying quiet feels worse than talking, so I— I filled the quiet. I always fill the quiet. I'm sorry."

Maren let it sit. Rain filled the gap where an answer should have gone.

"Green-shawl woman," she said.

"That's what she wanted. Not the writ. The woman in the green shawl." He wiped his face with his sleeve. "I didn't give a name. I don't have one to give. I swear it on my mother, and she's not even—"

"Later."

"Right. Later. Later is good. I like later—"

"Tovin." She tipped her head at the rope-seller's stall. "Stay. Count the rope."

He nodded too many times and started counting under his breath.

Maren crossed to the stall. The rope-seller was an old man with hands like knotted cable. He had a coil half-made and did not stop working it. He had watched all of it. Men like him always did. They sold rope and they sold silence, and the silence cost more.

"Need cord?" he said.

"Need a door."

That got his eyes up. Just for a moment.

She took the writ from inside her coat. Kept it low, under the awning line, out of the rain. Turned it so the red wax caught what light there was. A wren, pressed in the seal. Wings up. A ring around it.

The old man stopped working the coil.

"Where's the door with this on it," Maren said.

"Put that away."

"Where."

"Under your coat, girl, before someone with a badge sees you flashing Council wax like a fishwife flashing a coin." He set the coil down. Slow. His voice dropped. "You didn't get that from me."

"Got it from a Warden. She wants me to carry it. I said no."

His mouth worked. "Then you're smarter than you look. And you look smart enough."

"The door."

He looked down the row, both ways. The rain gave him cover to do it.

"Old quarter," he said. "Past the cooper's, where the lane runs to nothing. Black door. No handle on the street side. Wren over the lintel, cut in the stone, not painted. Council stock, that mark. Same as your wax."

"Opens for who?"

"People the harbor books don't get to see." He picked the coil back up. Wanted his hands busy. "Counting house. Or worse than a counting house. Things go in that door. Crates, mostly. Sometimes not crates."

"Not crates."

"I sell rope," he said. "I don't ask what they tie."

Maren looked at the writ. The wren looked back. Wings up. Going nowhere.

Her sister had sent three words on oilcloth. Salt Market. Low tide. Then three weeks of nothing. Now there was a green-shawl woman in Tovin's mouth and a Warden who wanted her at a black door by second bell. Maybe the same thread. Maybe not. Threads didn't care what you called them.

"Sable," she said. "The Warden. She uses that door?"

The old man went still again. "I said what I said. For the price of a coil I'll say no more, and you'll buy the coil, because a woman who takes free rope gets remembered."

Fair. Maren put coin on the boards. Didn't count it. Let him.

"Heads down," he said. "Second bell's coming. Whatever she told you about a room with a door — that's not the only door in this city with your name near it."

Tovin's voice came thin through the rain. "Maren. The tide. It's turning."

The water in the gutters was already climbing.

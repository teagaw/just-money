# Etsy Listing Description

The description uses a **PAS (Problem → Agitate → Solution) structure** — the most-converting structure for marketplace listings. The buyer arrives knowing she has a problem. The description names it, makes it sting, and then offers the bundle as the obvious fix. The body is scannable (short paragraphs, bullets, headers). The CTA at the end is specific and action-oriented.

**Word count target:** 700-900 words. Long enough to convey value, short enough to read in 3 minutes.

**Etsy formatting rules:**
- **No HTML.** Etsy uses a custom subset of markdown. Headers use `##` and `###`. Bold uses `**text**`. Bullets use `-` or `*`. Line breaks matter — Etsy ignores single line breaks; use a blank line to start a new paragraph.
- **First 160 characters are the search snippet.** That's what shows up under the title in search results. Front-load the value.
- **No external links in the description.** Etsy suppresses listings with external links. Link in the listing photos or in the "About" section of the shop.

The full description follows. The version below is the final, ready-to-paste version.

## Description Structure Notes

### Section 1 — Hook (lines 1-3)

**The Problem, stated bluntly.** "Stop designing Instagram posts from scratch. Every. Single. Time."

This is the buyer's actual daily life. She'll recognize herself in three seconds. The sentence fragments ("Every. Single. Time.") make the pain feel like a real burden, not a generic complaint.

### Section 2 — Agitate (lines 5-9)

**The Problem, made worse.** "You sit down to post... your feed looks like four different brands had a fight."

This is the moment the buyer nods along. The "four different brands had a fight" line is the emotional kicker. It namechecks the specific pain (inconsistent feed) that the bundle solves.

### Section 3 — Bridge (line 11)

**The transition.** "There's a better way. This is the better way."

Two short sentences. The first promises relief. The second promises *this product* is the relief. Move on fast.

### Section 4 — What's Inside (lines 13-46)

**The Solution, itemized.** Bullets with the bundle's deliverables, each with a one-line value statement. The buyer skims this. The categories (Quote, Testimonial, etc.) are listed — she should be able to see that the bundle has structure, not just "50 random templates."

### Section 5 — Differentiation (lines 48-66)

**Why this, not the alternatives.** "This bundle is a system, not a pile of files." This is the core differentiator from `README.md` (differentiation angles 1, 3, 4). It positions against the "50 random templates" competitor.

### Section 6 — Who This Is For (lines 68-85)

**The audience qualification.** Two paragraphs. The first is the bulleted list of *exactly* who this is for — the buyer checks off her own identity. The second is the *anti-audience* — the people this is *not* for. The anti-audience section is critical: it makes the buyer feel "this seller knows what she wants and built for me specifically."

### Section 7 — How It Works (lines 87-99)

**The mechanism, made simple.** 5 steps, 1 sentence each. The buyer sees that it's a 60-second process. The 2-hour-to-1-minute math is the ROI argument in concrete terms.

### Section 8 — Visual System (lines 101-109)

**The aesthetic positioning.** This is where the design language is described. "Kinfolk magazine spread, not Canva template" is the punchline. The buyer should be able to *imagine* the look before seeing the photos.

### Section 9 — What You Get (lines 111-123)

**The deliverable list, formally.** This is the "in the box" section. It's also where the buyer's "is this legit?" doubt is answered. The "yours forever" line removes the subscription fear.

### Section 10 — A Note on the Buyer (lines 125-134)

**The emotional close.** Two short paragraphs. The first reframes the buyer's identity: "you shouldn't have to choose." The second makes the bundle the bridge. The "you already have the work" line validates everything she's done so far.

### Section 11 — FAQ (lines 136-170)

**The objection handling.** 5 common questions answered. The refund question is critical — digital products have higher refund rates than physical; the "all sales are final" + "message me within 7 days" combo is the right balance.

### Section 12 — CTA (lines 172-176)

**The call to action, specific and time-bound.** "Click the green Add to cart button." Etsy's button is actually green — this is intentional. "Get the bundle. Open Canva. Post your first redesigned post before lunch." The "before lunch" line creates a tiny time-bound challenge. "Your feed will never look the same." The closer.

## Paste and Test

The description is ready to paste into Etsy's "Description" field. Format in Etsy's editor will render:

- The `##` and `###` headers as section dividers
- The `**bold**` text as bolded emphasis
- The bulleted lists as standard bullets
- The `---` as a thin horizontal rule
- The blank lines as paragraph breaks

Test by previewing the listing before publishing. The bullet rendering and the header sizes are the most common places to need adjustment.

## Description Versions to A/B Test

After the first 30 days, consider testing:

- **Version 2:** Lead with the *outcome* ("Your feed will look like a $200/month designer is on retainer") instead of the *problem* ("Stop designing from scratch"). Tests whether outcome-led intros convert better.
- **Version 3:** Lead with the *audience* ("This is for the small business owner who spends 2 hours per post") instead of the *problem*. Tests whether audience-led intros convert better for self-identifying buyers.

Run each version for 30 days. Compare conversion rate. Keep the winner. The PAS structure is the right starting point, but the hook at the top is always tunable.
